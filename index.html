Is the barrow a European thing?

Long barrows in Wessex, round barrows on the Yorkshire Wolds, bronze-age cairns in Sweden, kurgans on the steppe. I mapped 99,049 burial mounds and burial cairns across Europe, and dated the tradition with radiocarbon. Is there a barrow belt across the north? Or just a belt of good records?

Dr Tristan Hotham, Independent researcher · 2026 · Data: OpenStreetMap, Wikidata, p3k14c radiocarbon database, IntCal20, Natural Earth

Walk the chalk downs of southern England and they are everywhere: grassy humps on the skyline, some long, most round, the burial places of people who farmed here four or five thousand years ago. Barrows feel very British. Barrow is an old English word for a hill or grave mound, and "low", "howe" and "tump" are on the map too. But the Danes have their høje, the Swedes their rösen, the Germans their Hügelgräber, the Dutch their grafheuvels, and across the plains of Ukraine and southern Russia stand the kurgans.

So is the barrow a European thing? A north-west European thing? I pulled every burial mound and burial cairn I could find in OpenStreetMap and Wikidata, from Ireland to the Urals, 99,049 of them in Europe and 1,331 more in Turkey and the Caucasus, and linked radiocarbon dates to the barrows they came from. The answer is less tidy than the question, and more interesting: it depends what you count, and who did the counting.

The map of Europe's barrows is, first of all, a map of Europe's heritage registers.

01 · The mapA hundred thousand mounds

Every dot is one barrow, coloured by type. Filter by type, by period (where the record gives one), by country and by source, or switch to the density view: barrows per 1,000 km² in squares of 50 km. Tap a dot for its name and record.

Tap a dot to see the barrow's record, or a square in the density view for its count.

Look at the dots and the answer to the title seems obvious. The barrows crowd the north: 83% of them lie north of 50° N, which holds 62% of Europe's land, and 75% sit between 50° and 60° N, a band with 38% of the land. Denmark alone has 27,236, 638 for every 1,000 km². The ten densest 50 km squares in Europe are all in Denmark and Sweden.

02 · Who counted?Eight registers and a lot of volunteers

Then look at where the records came from. In Sweden, 28,844 of the 29,324 barrows are Wikidata items, 99% of them without even a name: items created in bulk from the national register of ancient monuments. In Denmark, OpenStreetMap holds 25,679, and the 22,673 Danish barrows in Wikidata sit a median of 3.6 m from an OpenStreetMap barrow (93% within 30 m), a sign that both were drawn from the same national list. Germany's Wikidata barrows are labelled with Bavarian monument numbers (95% of them) and 99% lie in Bavaria; most of Spain's are in the Basque Country (79%); Britain's carry the long descriptive names of the scheduled monument lists ("Bowl barrow on Huggate Wold, 500m NNW of Horsedale Plantation"); Finland, the Netherlands and Slovenia have their own sets.

I call a country "register-fed" where Wikidata holds at least 250 barrows and at least one per 1,000 km²: elsewhere Wikidata has almost none. Eight European countries pass: Sweden, Denmark, Germany, United Kingdom, Spain, Finland, Netherlands and Slovenia. They cover 20% of Europe's land and hold 92% of its mapped barrows, 46 per 1,000 km². Everywhere else the figure is 1.0: what volunteers have mapped by hand in OpenStreetMap.

Take the register-fed countries out and the northern tilt goes. In the other 43 countries, 41% of the barrows lie north of 50° N, on 61% of their land: if anything a southern lean, because Russia's vast and thinly mapped north weighs heavily. The densest band outside the registers is not the North Sea coast but central Europe, around 47° to 50° N: Austria, Hungary, Czechia, southern Poland, Bavaria's neighbours.

Barrows by latitude

Presence is a fairer test than density, because one barrow is enough to mark a square. Across Europe, 32% of 50 km squares hold at least one mapped barrow. In the register-free countries, 34% of squares south of 50° N have one, 22% between 50° and 60° N, and 4% north of 60° N. Burial mounds were built almost everywhere people farmed. What varies most is how thoroughly they have been written down.

03 · Earth or stoneThe mound lands and the stone coast

There is a measure that does not care how hard each country's mappers worked. In the same OpenStreetMap pull I also have every mapped megalith: dolmens, passage graves, menhirs, stone circles. For each country I can ask what share of its mapped prehistoric monuments are mounds rather than stones. A keen mapper adds both, so the share cancels much of the effort.

Mounds as a share of mapped mounds and megaliths

Megaliths: OpenStreetMap objects tagged archaeological_site=megalith that are not also barrows. Mounds over megalithic chambers are counted as mounds when they are tagged as tumuli.

Here a clear pattern appears. Along the Atlantic edge, prehistoric monuments are mostly stone: mounds are 5% of the mapped total in France, 6% in Portugal, 28% in Ireland and 38% in Italy. Across the North European Plain and east to the Black Sea, they are almost all earth: 97% in the Netherlands, 95% in Poland, 97% in Austria, 100% in Hungary, 100% in Ukraine. Britain (69%) and Sweden (73%) sit in between, with both. So if there is a "barrow Europe", it is not the north-west fringe; it is the great lowland belt from the North Sea to the steppe, with the megalith builders of the Atlantic coast on its western flank.

04 · What kind?Long, round, cairn, kurgan

Most records just say tumulus: 69% are mounds of no stated type. The named types tell their own story about who records what. Round barrows (4,998) are almost all British, because Historic England names its bowl, bell and disc barrows. Burial cairns (23,871) belong to the stony north and west: Sweden (15,606), Britain (4,963), Finland (2,368). Long barrows and long cairns are rare in the data (383), and are British, Danish and German. Kurgans, by name, number 1,171, and 90% lie east of 20° E.

05 · The steppeThe other heartland, almost unmapped

The Pontic and Caspian steppe should be the great exception: this is where the Yamnaya and their successors raised kurgans over their dead from the late fourth millennium BC, often in groups. In a box from 28° to 50° E and 44° to 52° N, I find 2,587 mapped barrows, 2.2 per 1,000 km², with at least one in 31% of the 50 km squares. 99.5% come from OpenStreetMap; Wikidata has almost none for Russia or Ukraine. 794 are named kurgans or mogilas, and 359 are single records for a whole group or cemetery of mounds.

That last number matters. A Russian mapper writing "Kurgan group 1 near the village" adds one point for what may be dozens of mounds, while a Danish register adds every mound. The data cannot tell us whether the steppe is the other heartland of the barrow. It can tell us that the steppe has nothing like a national register in OpenStreetMap or Wikidata, and that any count of Europe's barrows that leaves this out is a count of records, not of mounds.

06 · When?A Bronze Age habit, with Neolithic roots

To date the tradition I used the p3k14c database of radiocarbon dates, which holds 77,532 dates in Europe. I kept the dates whose site name says barrow, tumulus, cairn, kurgan, høj, hög, Grabhügel or one of their cousins, and calibrated them with IntCal20: 489 dates from 132 sites. To stop one heavily sampled site from drowning the rest, each site's dates share a weight of one.

Radiocarbon dates from barrows, by region
Curves: summed calibrated probability, each site weighted equally, scaled to each region's peak. Ticks: the median calibrated date of each sample. Hover or tap a tick for the site.
Calibration: IntCal20. Shaded bands mark rough period boundaries used in the text, not regional chronologies.

The dates fall in two waves. The first is Neolithic: 33% of the site-weighted dates are older than 2500 BC, and in Britain and Ireland the summed curve has a first peak around 3650 BC, the age of the long barrows. The dates from barrows named as long barrows or long cairns have a median of 3640 BC, and 93% are older than 2500 BC. The second wave is bigger: 59% of the dates fall between 2500 and 800 BC, with Britain's peak near 1790 BC, the age of the round barrows. Only 8% are later than 800 BC.

The wider set, adding 254 more sites whose dates were taken within 100 m of a mapped barrow, tells the same story: 32% before 2500 BC and 55% between 2500 and 800 BC. The radiocarbon record is overwhelmingly British and Scandinavian, though: 70 and 37 named sites, against 6 in eastern Europe and the steppe. And it is blind to later mounds. Iron Age and Viking barrows are usually dated by what was buried in them, a brooch or a sword, so they are not here in proportion. Where the map records give a period (6% of them), the Bronze Age leads with 52% of those with a specific period, then the Iron Age with 32%, the Neolithic 7% and early medieval or Viking mounds 6%; most of those periods come from Finland's register, which dates its cairns to the Bronze and Iron Ages.

07 · So?Is the barrow a European thing?

Mound burial is a European thing in the plain sense: there are mapped barrows in 40 of 51 European countries, from Ireland to the Urals, and the dated ones cluster in the Neolithic and above all the Bronze Age. It is not only European, of course: the same Wikidata classes hold 1,400 Japanese kofun, and burial mounds were raised in Asia and the Americas too. But 98% of the Wikidata barrows with coordinates lie in my European box, which says more about who builds Wikidata than about who built mounds.

Is it a north-west European thing? The raw map says yes, emphatically. The sources say be careful: the barrow belt across Denmark, southern Sweden, the Netherlands, northern Germany and Britain is real on the ground, but its height on the map is the height of a few national registers. The pattern that survives every test is broader: an earthen mound tradition across the lowlands from the North Sea to the steppe, framed by stone tombs on the Atlantic coast, and a peak of building between about 2500 and 800 BC.

08 · LimitsWhat this can and cannot say

  • Records, not mounds. These are the barrows that someone has put into OpenStreetMap or Wikidata. Ploughed-out mounds, unrecorded ones and most of the steppe's kurgans are missing. Compare shares and patterns within the data, not raw totals between countries.
  • Registers make the map. 92% of the records come from eight countries with bulk imports from heritage registers. Germany's Wikidata barrows are almost all from Bavaria's monument list. I report these countries separately wherever I can.
  • One point, many mounds. 1,089 records name a group or cemetery rather than one mound, most of them in Russia and Britain. They undercount.
  • Types are what the record says. A Danish "høj" may be a Bronze Age mound over a Neolithic grave. I classify by tags, Wikidata class and name, so "round barrow" is mostly a British label and "kurgan" an eastern one.
  • Dates are from barrow sites, not of barrow building. A date from a barrow may come from a buried body, a later burial dug into the mound, or charcoal from before it was raised. Linking is by site name (and, in the wider set, by distance), and the dated sites are mostly British and Scandinavian. Later mounds are under-dated by radiocarbon.
  • Positions are approximate. OpenStreetMap areas are placed at the first point of their outline, not the centre: fine at map scale.

How I did this

Barrows. From a pull of 65,670 OpenStreetMap objects in a Europe box (25° W to 60° E, 34° to 72° N; QLever, 27 October 2026), I kept those tagged archaeological_site=tumulus or cairn, tomb=tumulus, or megalith_type long_barrow, round_barrow, barrow, chambered_cairn, ring_cairn or cairn: 47,826 objects, 47,664 after dropping 162 within 5 m of another (node and outline of one mound). From Wikidata I took every item of class tumulus (and its subclasses, such as bowl barrow) or cairn (except clearance cairns) with coordinates in the box: 76,436. I dropped 5,136 that an OpenStreetMap barrow already links and 18,533 within 30 m of one, leaving 52,767.

Countries and squares. Each barrow is placed in a Natural Earth country (or the nearest within 10 km). Europe is Natural Earth's European countries clipped at 60° E, so Russia up to the Urals; Turkey, Cyprus, Georgia, Armenia, Azerbaijan and Kazakhstan west of 60° E are shown as neighbours. Densities use land area in an equal-area projection (ETRS89 Lambert azimuthal, EPSG:3035), and 50 km squares on the same grid; squares with less than 250 km² of land are left out of the rankings.

Types and periods. Type comes first from the record's tags and Wikidata class, then from words in its name (long barrow, Hünenbett, langdysse; kurgan, курган, mogila; cairn, röse; bowl barrow and so on). Period comes from OpenStreetMap's historic:period and historic:civilization tags and Wikidata's period and culture, mapped to six broad bins.

Dates. From p3k14c (Bird and others, 2022), the dates whose site name contains a barrow word, after removing burnt mounds, towns and streets called Barrow, cairnfields, dolmens, samples described as under or next to a barrow, and eight names that are not barrows. Dates with errors above 300 years and marine shell were dropped. Each date was calibrated against IntCal20 on a one-year grid (lab and curve errors added in quadrature). Every linked date, and the rule that linked it, is in barrow_dates.csv.
